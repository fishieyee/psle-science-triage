PSLE Science in 11 Days
A triage plan

For a child who struggles to remember and has gaps in the basics

Prepared 18 September 2026  |  Science paper: Tuesday 29 September, 08:15

01Start with the honest position

In 11 days you cannot rebuild a child's understanding of Science from scratch, and trying will make things worse — she will meet failure on every topic and lose confidence right before the exam.

What you can do is different, and it is worth doing:

  • Secure six core ideas that sit behind roughly half the paper, taught with something she can see and touch.
  • Learn ten answer frames — sentence shapes she can reuse, so she is not starting from a blank page.
  • Stop the technique losses, which are often 10–15 marks for a child like this: blanks left empty, one point written where two were needed, units missing.

The goal is not to cover the syllabus. The goal is to convert the marks that are already within reach and to stop her freezing. A child who currently writes "I don't know" can realistically pick up marks on the 1- and 2-mark parts, which make up most of Booklet B.

What to say to her

"We are not going to learn everything. We are going to learn six things really well, and I promise you will be able to answer more questions than you think." A child who believes she can improve studies; a child who thinks she is hopeless does not.

02The one change that matters

Stop re-reading and stop memorising lists. Start attaching every fact to a reason.

Memory fails when facts are floating and unconnected — which is exactly why "very weak at remembering" and "doesn't understand the basic concept" are the same problem, not two. She is not forgetting because her memory is bad. She is forgetting because there is nothing to hang the fact on.

So apply this rule for the next 11 days. For every fact she is asked to remember, she must be able to finish the sentence "because…". If she cannot, do not memorise it — drop it. Dropping it is the right decision. A small number of understood facts beats a large number of half-remembered ones.

Practical test

Ask her to explain one idea to you out loud, in her own words, as if you were the student. If she can, she knows it. If she goes quiet or recites phrases that don't connect, she doesn't — and that is your signal to spend more time there, not to move on. Teach-back is the fastest test of understanding there is.

03The six ideas to secure

Each of these has a two-minute demonstration. Do the demonstration first, then let her say the idea in her own words. Do not explain it before she has seen it.

1

Heat moves from hot to cold, until both reach the same temperature

Two-minute demo

Put a metal spoon and a wooden spoon in a mug of hot water. Wait one minute. Touch both. The metal one feels much hotter — heat travelled through the metal quickly and slowly through the wood.

Answers questions about: why a metal tap feels colder than a wooden door; why a cold drink can gets wet on the outside; why metal pans have wooden or plastic handles; why wires snap on a cold night.

2

Heat gain or loss changes state — and while it is changing, the temperature stays the same

Two-minute demo

Ice cubes in a pot with a thermometer, on low heat. Watch the thermometer sit at 0 °C while the ice melts, and only start climbing once all the ice is gone.

This is not a warm-up exercise — the graph showing a flat line during a change of state is explicitly part of the 2026 syllabus. Seeing it happen once is worth more than reading about it ten times.

Answers questions about: the flat part of a temperature–time graph; the water cycle; why wet clothes dry faster on a windy day.

3

Energy is transferred and converted — it is never used up or lost

Two-minute demo

Switch on a torch for five minutes, then feel the bulb — it is warm. Electrical energy became light and heat. Then roll a ball down a ramp: it never climbs back to the same height on the other side, because some energy went into heat and sound.

Answers questions about: every "state the energy conversion" question; why a toy slows down; renewable and non-renewable energy resources.

4

Current only flows around a complete loop

Two-minute demo

A torch with the battery pushed slightly out — nothing happens. Push it in — light. Open the torch and take the bulb out — nothing. Put it back — light.

Then say the key sentence out loud: break the loop anywhere and everything in that loop stops. That single sentence answers most circuit questions.

Answers questions about: why a bulb doesn't light; why the other bulb stays lit in a parallel circuit; conductors and insulators; electromagnets.

5

Friction opposes motion

Two-minute demo

Push a coin across a table, then across a towel. Then rub your palms together hard for ten seconds — they get warm. Friction slows things down and turns motion into heat.

Answers questions about: why a ball stops sooner on a rough floor; why we oil machine parts; why shoes have grip; why a slide gets hot.

6

Plants make food from light, carbon dioxide and water — and all living things need oxygen to release the energy in food

Two-minute demo

Use a recipe. Three ingredients: light, carbon dioxide, water. That is all the 2026 syllabus requires — chlorophyll is no longer a required keyword.

Then the second half: food is the fuel, oxygen is what it burns in. Animals cannot make their own food, so they eat plants or other animals — which is exactly what a food chain shows.

Answers questions about: photosynthesis; plant transport; food chains and webs; why animals depend on plants.

04Ten answer frames to memorise

This is the highest-value thing in this document. Do not memorise facts — memorise these ten sentence shapes. She fills in the brackets with whatever the question is about. The same frame works across dozens of questions.

1 · Condensation The warm water vapour lost heat to the cooler [surface], condensing into water droplets.
2 · Evaporation The water gained heat and evaporated, changing from a liquid to water vapour.
3 · Expansion The [object] gained heat and expanded, so [result].
4 · Contraction while fixed in place The [object] lost heat and contracted. As it was fixed tightly, it [stretched / snapped].
5 · Friction The frictional force between [A] and [B] is greater. Friction acts in the opposite direction to the motion, so [object] slows down more and travels a shorter distance.
6 · Energy conversion chain [form] energy → [form] energy → [form] energy.
7 · Fair test To make it a fair test, [variable] must be kept the same.
8 · Why repeat an experiment The experiment was repeated to obtain more reliable results.
9 · Describing a relationship from data (2 marks) As [X] increases, [Y] increases until [value]. Beyond that, [Y] decreases.
10 · Parallel circuit Current can still flow through the other path, which is still a closed circuit, so the other bulb remains lit.
How to use these

Have her write all ten out by hand, in her own words, on one sheet of paper. Do not write it for her — the act of writing it is the memorising. Then she reads only that sheet on the morning of the exam. Handwriting it herself also means the wording is hers, which means she can actually reproduce it under pressure.

06Multiple choice technique — this is now 60 marks

Under the 2026 format, Booklet A is 30 questions worth 2 marks each — 60 of the 100 marks. It used to be 56. Multiple choice is no longer the easy part, and it is where technique pays fastest.

  • Never leave a blank. No marks are deducted for a wrong answer. A guess is always better than an empty box — but guess by eliminating first, not randomly.
  • Read the last line of the question first. It tells her what she is actually being asked, so she reads the paragraph with a purpose.
  • Cross out anything with the wrong unit. Millimetres where centimetres were asked, degrees where centimetres were asked.
  • Watch for "increase in" versus the plain value. A question asking for the increase in length is not asking for the length. This trap caught students in 2024.
  • If two options both look right, find the one word that differs between them. That word is the whole question.
  • For graph questions, read the axis labels and units before looking at the line. Most graph errors are axis errors.
  • Ignore the story. When the question is about otah, a toy boat or a football match, the science is still one of the six ideas. Find the science words and ignore the rest.
  • Budget about 45 minutes for Booklet A, then move on. Do not let one question eat five minutes.
New in 2026

The paper now includes linked questions — up to two pairs of questions based on the same scenario or experiment. If she misreads the shared context, she can lose both marks in a pair. Tell her: when two questions in a row look related, re-read the setup once before answering the second one.

07Booklet B technique

  • The number of marks tells her the number of points. A [2] question needs two ideas; a [3] needs three. If she has written one point for a [2] question, she has already lost a mark.
  • Underline the command word. State means one line. Describe means what happens. Explain means she must use because or so. An explanation without a because is not an explanation.
  • Always write something. A single correct keyword can earn a mark. An empty line earns nothing, ever.
  • Use the answer frames. Same sentence shape, new words. This is why they are worth memorising — they remove the blank-page problem.
  • Short and scientific, not long and story-like. Booklet B rewards precision, not length.
  • Budget about 50 minutes, and keep the last 10 minutes to check that no part has been left blank.

08On the day

  • Report by 07:45. The paper starts at 08:15 and runs 1 hour 45 minutes.
  • Pack the night before: 2B pencil, soft eraser, dark blue or black pen, ruler. Pencil for shading the multiple-choice options, pen for written answers.
  • She reads only her own one-page sheet in the morning. No new material, no past papers.
  • If she gets stuck, mark the question and move on. Come back at the end. Panic costs more marks than ignorance.
  • There will be at least one question about a situation she has never seen. That is normal and deliberate. The answer is still one of the six ideas.
  • A proper night's sleep beats an extra hour of revision. This is not a platitude — recall collapses without sleep.

09What not to do

Avoid these
  • Don't buy new assessment books now. More material is the opposite of what she needs.
  • Don't make her memorise topic lists. That is the approach that has already failed her.
  • Don't sit with her for three hours. 60–90 minutes, then stop, even if it is going well.
  • Don't correct every mistake during practice. Let her finish, then review. Constant interruption stops her from thinking.
  • Don't do a full paper every day. One timed paper, then repair what it revealed.
  • Don't introduce new topics after 27 September. Consolidation beats coverage in the final days.
  • Don't say "you should know this" or "we did this already." If she knew it, she would not be asking. This sentence teaches her to hide what she doesn't know, which is the worst possible outcome one week before an exam.
Praise the process, not the result

"That explanation was clear" works. "You're so clever" does not — it makes her afraid of the next question in case it proves otherwise. When she gets something wrong, the useful question is not "why didn't you know that?" but "what do you think happens first?" Get her talking, then correct. Silence is the thing to fear, not wrong answers.
